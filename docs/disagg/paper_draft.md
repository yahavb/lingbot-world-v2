# [SYSTEM]: Role-Disaggregated Serving for Two-Stage Generative Pipelines (Operational Systems)

## Abstract

Generative models for interactive video, world simulation and long-form audio share one pipeline:

- a diffusion transformer (DiT) denoises latents;
- a learned decoder (a VAE) turns them into pixels or samples.

Serving stacks co-locate both stages on the same accelerators. We show that this coupling limits throughput per
chip, for a different reason than in LLM prefill/decode disaggregation. On AWS Trainium3:

- the DiT is bound by on-chip data movement;
- the decoder is bound by systolic-array utilization;
- so they scale differently with chips, and co-location runs each stage at the other's chip count, serially.

We give a cost model of this coupling that predicts all 15 measured configurations of a 14B world model within
1.4%. We build [SYSTEM] on Amazon EKS: denoiser and decoder are roles that are sized, replicated, placed and failed
over independently, using DRA claims with paired EFA, NIXL device-to-device writes (0.17 ms per latent) and
per-session decoder state.

On LingBot-World v2 and Stable Audio 3, [SYSTEM]:

- reaches 1.20x the frame rate of the best 16-chip co-located configuration (32.1 vs 26.7 fps);
- serves 720p where an 8-chip co-located replica does not fit;
- runs 3 streams per 1-chip decoder with no slowdown, and 9 streams on 3 decoders;
- matches co-located median press-to-photon latency and beats its p90.

## 1 Introduction

**Background.**

- Interactive world models turn actions into video chunk by chunk.
- Robot-policy training runs many such simulations in parallel.
- Audio models render long pieces in seconds.

Every unit of output passes through a DiT and then a decoder.

**Problem.** Serving stacks inherit the LLM playbook: tensor and sequence parallelism, and the whole pipeline
co-located in each replica. These pipelines have two stages, and neither is negligible:

- in a 14B world model on 16 Trainium3 chips, the decoder takes 143 ms per chunk next to the DiT's 300 ms;
- in Stable Audio 3, decode is 40-53% of the DiT's time.

**Motivation: two stages, two usage profiles (Figure 3, bounds.svg).** The DiT and the VAE use the accelerator very
differently (all measured, LingBot, 4 chips, 480x768 unless noted):

| Typical usage | DiT | VAE |
|---|---|---|
| Matmul engine busy | 68% of the time | 88% |
| Data movement busy (overlaps compute) | 82% | 51% |
| Talks to other chips each call | yes: TP and CP | barely: tile edges only |
| Device memory per rank | about 8.4 GiB of weights plus an 8.65 GB KV cache (720p) | about 3 GiB, with 4 sessions |
| Speed-up from 8x the chips | 3.2x | 2.1x |

The DiT is big, spreads over several chips and talks between them. The VAE is small and compute-dense, and fits one
chip with room to share.

**Why co-location is suboptimal.** A co-located replica runs both stages on the same chips, one after the other:

- every chip runs the DiT, then every chip runs the VAE;
- so the VAE is charged at the DiT's chip count, where it gains little: at 16 chips it holds 2,032 chip-ms per chunk,
  against 324 on its own chip (Figure chiptime);
- and each stage waits for the other.

**The claim.** Splitting the stages into independently sized and scaled roles gives **about 27% higher frame rate on
average** (measured over four configurations), and serves a real-time stream on **44% fewer chips**. The per-setup
breakdown is in section 7:

| Setup | fps gain |
|---|---|
| LingBot 720p, 16 chips | 11% |
| LingBot 480, 16 chips | 20% |
| Stable Audio, 190 s windows | 32-36% |
| LingBot, 2-step DiT | 44% |
| Stable Audio, 280 s windows | **[to validate: not yet measured]** |

Once the 280 s run is measured, the average is expected to be about 30% [to validate]. The gain is larger when the VAE
is a bigger share of the work, as the cost model predicts (section 4).

**Why not prefill/decode disaggregation.** That design separates a compute-bound phase from a bandwidth-bound one
that interfere. Our profiles show neither: the two stages have different bounds, and they interact through time and
chip count (section 3).

**Challenges.**

1. **Finding the binding resource of each stage.** The classic roofline places both far from compute and bandwidth
   alike.
2. **Choosing the chip split and the replica ratio.** The right answer depends on model, resolution and steps, and a
   wrong split loses (8 chips).
3. **Sharing a stateful decoder.** A causal VAE carries per-stream state across chunks.
4. **Operating two roles on Kubernetes.** Device and EFA pairing, latent transport between device memories, pod
   discovery, failover.

**Our work.** Contributions:

1. **A characterization of the two stages on Trainium3,** with an extended roofline. The DiT is bound by on-chip data
   movement (DMA busy 82%, 8.8x its HBM need). The decoder is bound by array utilization (tensor busy about 90% at
   44-50% of peak).
2. **A cost model of stage coupling:** T_d(N) + T_v(N) co-located, max(T_d(n_d), T_v(n_v)) disaggregated, and
   k = T_d / T_v streams per decoder. It predicts 15 measured configurations within 1.4%, and a held-out 16-chip DiT
   within 2.5%.
3. **[SYSTEM], role disaggregation as an operating-system abstraction on EKS.** It has DRA claims with paired EFA,
   NIXL writes between device memories, per-session decoder caches, least-loaded placement and failover.
4. **An evaluation on two pipelines.** It covers chip budgets, resolutions, denoising steps, N:M ratios, latency,
   ablations and failures, including where disaggregation does not pay.

## 2 Background

**2.1 Two-stage generative pipelines.**

- **LingBot-World v2 (14B, causal video).**
  - Chunks of 3 latent frames, 12 video frames each.
  - 4 distilled denoising steps plus a KV-cache commit pass.
  - A rolling KV cache: a sink chunk plus 4 chunks.
  - A causal 3-D convolutional VAE with a per-stream temporal cache.
  - 480x768, or 720p (704x1280).
- **Stable Audio 3 Medium.**
  - 380 s windows, each inpainted from the previous window's last 60 s, which must be decoded and re-encoded first.
  - A chunked, stateless decoder.

**2.2 Trainium3 and the serving cluster.**

- 16 chips per trn3-dev1 node, 4 logical cores per chip at LNC2.
- Each core has tensor, vector, scalar and GpSimd engines, plus DMA queues, which run concurrently.
- EFA between nodes, NeuronLink within a node.
- Kubernetes (EKS) with the Neuron DRA driver.

**Table 1: metrics.**

| Metric | Definition |
|---|---|
| fps per stream | client-side frame rate over 60 chunks after 5 warm chunks |
| streams per chip | concurrent streams / chips used, all roles |
| press-to-photon | key press to the first frame generated under that action, at the client |
| T_d(n), T_v(n) | per-unit time of the DiT and decoder on n chips (a 12-frame chunk, or an audio window) |
| floors | per graph: FLOPs / peak, HBM bytes / bandwidth, tensor busy, DMA busy, serial time |

## 3 Characterization: what bounds each stage

**3.1 Binding resources (Figure 3).** From a system trace of a warm run, we take a device profile of every graph
that carries the latency, and compute one floor per resource.

**Two usage profiles (Figure 3, bounds.svg).** Typical usage of each stage, all measured:

| | DiT | VAE |
|---|---|---|
| Matmul engine busy | 68% of the time | 88% |
| Data movement busy (overlaps compute) | 82% | 51% |
| Talks to other chips each call | yes: TP and CP, about 25% of its data movement | barely: tile edges only |
| Device memory per rank | about 8.4 GiB of weights plus an 8.65 GB KV cache (720p) | about 3 GiB, with 4 sessions |
| Speed-up from 8x the chips | 3.2x (2 to 16 chips) | 2.1x (1 to 8 chips) |

The DiT is big, spreads over several chips and talks between them; the VAE is small and compute-dense, and fits one
chip with room to share. Engine shares are from the main program of each stage (4 chips, 480x768); memory from the
pods' allocation logs.

**Coverage.** Share of chip-busy time in the profiled run (4 chips, 480x768, about 31.7 s of chip time), source
perf/disagg/results/prof/p14_devprof_dma_480.devprof.txt:

| Group | Share of chip time | Class |
|---|---|---|
| DiT attention blocks (2 programs) | 76% | on-chip movement |
| Other DiT programs (4) | 11% | on-chip movement |
| VAE convolutions (4) | 7% | array utilization |
| 17 small programs, each under 1% | 6% | not profiled |

The 10 profiled programs cover 94% of the chip time, and each one falls into one of the two classes. The figure shows
the largest program of each stage.

We observe that:

- **The DiT's attention blocks are not compute-bound or HBM-bound.** Their DMA queues are busy 82% of the time
  (5.25 of 6.38 ms) while their HBM floor is 0.60 ms. The excess is on-chip movement: MXFP8 activation transposes,
  dynamic gathers (about half of DMA time), and transfers averaging 157 KB. The tensor engine reaches 28% of peak.
- **The decoder's halo convolutions keep the tensor engine busy about 90% of the time,** but at 44-50% of the FLOPs
  it could deliver. The convolution shapes under-fill the systolic array. Their HBM floor is 0.1-0.3 ms.
- **Where the DiT's DMA time goes** (main attention block, 114.6 ms of DMA time summed over its queues per call):
  - 37% (42.1 ms, 737 MB): SBUF-to-SBUF transposes issued by the MXFP8 activation loader before every MXFP8 matrix
    multiply;
  - about 25%: small collective transfers (TP/CP synchronization) that move little data but wait long;
  - about 5%: remote SBUF reads between the two physical cores of an LNC2 logical core.

  The DiT re-lays out its own activations on the chip; it is not starved for HBM bandwidth.
- **The transposes are the price of MXFP8's input layout on this kernel.** nkilib's matmul_mxfp8 takes the activation
  token-major and converts it to its own layout on every call. Both cheaper variants we know of were measured, and
  both were slower:
  - passing the activation K-major, so the kernel skips its transpose, was slower for every shape (e.g. QKV 237 vs
    132 us);
  - quantizing the shared Q/K/V input once and reusing it cost more than it saved (467 vs 358 us).

  A cheaper path needs a different kernel design or a future SDK. It does not change our argument: the bound is real,
  correctly attributed, and different from the decoder's.
- **Per-chunk ceilings at bf16 peak:** about 155 ms for the DiT and about 58 ms for the decoder, against 552 and
  128 ms measured (4 chips).

**3.2 Scaling (Figure 2).** We observe that:

- **The DiT scales:** 1192, 686, 464 and 375 ms per chunk on 2, 4, 8 and 16 chips, 3.2x in all.
- **A decoder pod scales weakly:** 324, 213, 173 and 153 ms on 1, 2, 4 and 8 chips, 2.1x.
- **On the DiT's chips the decoder is flat:** 151, 133 and 143 ms at 4, 8 and 16.
- **Stable Audio's DiT stops sharding at 2 chips** (TP16 fails), while its decode scales 1.9x from 1 to 2 chips.

**How the two figures fit together.**

- **Figure bounds is the cause.** Each stage wastes time on a different thing: the DiT on moving data inside the
  chip, the VAE on a half-empty math engine. So they scale differently with chips. It also shows a per-kernel
  opportunity, but disaggregation does not take it: that is kernel work.
- **Figure chiptime is the effect.** It shows what disaggregation saves: 42% of the chip time per chunk, mostly by not
  spreading the VAE over the DiT's 16 chips.

**3.3 What disaggregation changes, and what it does not (Figure chiptime).**

Disaggregation does not make either stage's kernels faster: in the split, the DiT block still has 4.5 ms of headroom in its
6.4 ms and the convolution 3.2 ms in its 5.7. What it cuts is chip time per chunk, the chips held busy or waiting for
each 12 frames:

| Chip time per chunk | Co-located, 16 chips | Split, 8 + 1 |
|---|---|---|
| DiT stage | 4786 chip-ms | 2976 chip-ms |
| VAE stage | 2032 chip-ms | 324 chip-ms |
| Waiting and overhead | 368 chip-ms | 894 chip-ms |
| Total | 7186 chip-ms (26.7 fps) | 4194 chip-ms (25.8 fps) |

The split uses 42% less chip time per chunk at 96% of the frame rate. Most of the saving is the VAE: co-location multiplies
the VAE's waste by the DiT's chip count, and the split stops doing that. The per-kernel waste remains; it is
separate kernel work.

**3.4 The cost of co-location.** A co-located replica runs every chip through the DiT, then every chip through the
decoder. At 16 chips the decoder takes 16 × 143 ms = 2.29 chip-seconds per chunk. On its own chip it takes 0.32.

## 4 The cost model and rate matching

**Period per unit of work:**

- co-located: P_co = T_d(N) + T_v(N)
- disaggregated: P_dis = max(T_d(n_d), T_v(n_v)) + ε, where ε = 0.17 ms per 1.1 MB latent

**Rate matching.** One decoder replica serves k = T_d(n_d) / T_v(n_v) denoiser replicas.

- Beyond k, add decoder replicas and spread the sessions.
- When T_v > T_d, a denoiser needs more decoder capacity: bigger replicas, or more replicas for stateless decoders.

**Accuracy (Figure 4).** We observe that:

- **From per-stage times alone,** the model predicts all 15 measured LingBot configurations at 480 within 1.4%.
- **At 720p** it implies a 258 ms inline decoder at 16 chips, against 254 measured.
- **For streams per decoder** it predicts 3.7 and 2.1, against 3 and 2 that run without slowdown.
- **Extrapolation:** T_d(n) = a/n + b + c log2 n, fitted on 2, 4 and 8 chips, predicts 385 ms at 16 (375 measured).

**Deploy-time sizing.** Steps, resolution, window length and model are fixed per service, so T_d and T_v are known
before serving, and the operator computes n_d, n_v and the ratio once. When a service mixes configurations, each
role's pool resizes on its own. A co-located replica cannot change its ratio.

## 5 [SYSTEM] design

**5.1 Overview (Figure 5).** How a session is served:

1. The browser opens a session with lbw-web, the session manager.
2. lbw-web sends it through the DiT Service, so any Ready DiT pod takes it. The session is bound to that DiT pod
   (it holds the session's KV cache, which cannot be rebuilt mid-stream); a DiT pod serves one session at a time.
3. That DiT streams each chunk's latent to any Ready VAE pod with a free slot. The link is effectively sessionless:
   normally a session stays on its VAE, whose decoder cache keeps consecutive chunks smooth, but any VAE can take over
   at any chunk. When a VAE dies the session moves within about 3 s with no lost chunks; the new VAE starts a fresh
   decoder cache, at worst a small visual blip.
4. The frames go back through lbw-web to the browser.

Each VAE pod has K open slots, so it serves up to K sessions; their chunks are decoded one after another, each with its
own causal cache. About T_d / T_v sessions keep up. DiT pods scale out for more sessions; VAE pods scale out for more
open slots.

**Previous 5.1 text:**

- Two roles: a denoiser (DiT + T5) and a decoder (VAE). Each is a pool of replicas, sized, replicated, placed and
  failed over on its own.
- A session lives on one denoiser replica and one decoder replica.
- Each chunk's latent moves from denoiser device memory to decoder device memory.

**5.2 Resources: DRA claims with paired EFA.**

- Each Neuron device is claimed with its own EFA.
- Two-device claims take a directly linked pair (same topology_x); other pairs fail collective setup.
- Larger claims are counts inside one device group. Per-device constraints at 16 devices timed out the scheduler.

**5.3 Latent transport: NIXL between device memories.**

- One-sided writes over EFA (LIBFABRIC, FI_HMEM_NEURON) into a session's block of 4 receive slots, with a
  per-chunk notification tagged with the session id.
- Memory is registered by its runtime device address.
- A dead peer fails only the transfer in flight.

**5.4 Stateful decoders shared across streams.**

- A decoder replica serves up to K sessions.
- Each session's causal cache (427 MiB per rank) is swapped in before its chunk and saved after.
- Frames are byte-identical whether a stream decodes alone or shared.

**5.5 Placement and failover.**

- Each new session links to the least-loaded Ready decoder found by pod discovery (GET /load).
- A failed write sets the decoder aside for 60 s and retries the chunk on another.
- With no decoder available, the session is refused (503).

**5.6 Putting it all together.**

- The operator measures T_d(n) and T_v(n) once per service configuration, then sizes the roles from section 4.
- Discovery, placement and failover handle replicas joining and leaving.

## 6 Implementation and discussion

**Implementation.**

- The roles are serve.py (denoiser) and vae_serve.py (decoder); also wan/vae_pool.py, wan/nixl_link.py, and
  Kubernetes manifests with DRA ResourceClaimTemplates.
- Measurement tools: perf/disagg/*.
- Coding agents were used to build and measure the system. [Authors: the disclosure statement.]

**Where disaggregation does not pay.**

- Splitting a fixed budget can starve the stage that scales: LingBot at 8 chips gives 20.0 fps co-located against
  17.5 split; Stable Audio at 2 chips gives 6.15 s co-located against 7.50 s for 1+1.
- With many denoising steps the DiT dominates: Stable Audio at 25-50 steps gains 3-5%.

**Portability.** Nothing in the model is specific to Trainium: it needs per-stage scaling curves and a transport
for small latents. Only Trainium3 was measured; a second accelerator is future work.

## 7 Evaluation

**7.1 Methodology.**

- **Models:** LingBot-World v2 14B (480x768, 720p), and Stable Audio 3 Medium (1800 s of audio, 380 s and 190 s
  windows).
- **Hardware:** 2 trn3-dev1 nodes (32 chips), Neuron SDK 2.33, LNC2, MXFP8 DiT, bf16 decoder.
- **Baselines:**
  - co-located serving with the same model flags;
  - the same disaggregated split inside one pod (two containers on disjoint chips);
  - a 1-chip decoder vs larger decoders.
- **Workloads:**
  - LingBot: one live stream per DiT, the scene's camera path, 60 chunks.
  - Latency: 100 key presses.
  - Stable Audio: 2 warm runs per configuration.

**7.2 End-to-end performance (Figure 6, Table 2).**

Table 2: LingBot 480x768, fps.

| Co-located | fps | Disaggregated | fps |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 chips | 15.5 | 2+1, 2+2 | 10.1 |
| 8 chips | 22.6 | 4+1, 4+2, 4+4 | 17.5 |
| 16 chips | 26.7 | 8+1 / 8+2 / 8+4 / 8+8 | 25.8 / 26.0 / 25.5 / 25.6 |
| - | - | 16+1 / 16+2 / 16+4 | 32.1 / 32.2 / 32.2 |

We observe that:

- **At 16 chips the split wins.** 8+1 reaches 96% of co-located 16's frame rate on 9 chips instead of 16, and 16+1 is 1.20x faster.
- **At 8 chips co-located wins** (22.6 vs 17.5 for 4+x), because the split starves the DiT; 8+1 on 9 chips gives 25.7.
- **The decoder's size does not change fps:** a 1-chip decoder keeps up.
- **At 720p,** co-located 8 chips does not fit in device memory while the 8-chip DiT alone runs (8.5 fps), and 16+1
  gives 1.11x (12.7 vs 11.4 fps).

**7.3 Throughput vs interactivity (Figure 7).** Each configuration is plotted as streams per chip against fps per
stream.

- With N:M sharing, the disaggregated points reach 0.43 streams per chip at 10 fps (3 streams per 1-chip decoder).
- Co-located configurations reach at most 0.25 streams per chip at 14-15.5 fps.
- **Cost at real time (at least 16 fps per stream), in chips per stream:**
  - best co-located: 8 chips (20.0 fps);
  - best disaggregated: two 4-chip DiTs sharing one 1-chip decoder, 9 chips for 2 streams, so 4.5 chips per stream
    (17.5 fps each).
  - That is 44% fewer chip-hours per real-time stream-hour. Cost is chip-hours times the price per chip-hour; no
    dollar price is assumed.

**7.4 Rate matching and horizontal scaling (Figure 8).** We observe that:

- **One 1-chip decoder** serves 1, 2 and 3 streams of 2-chip DiTs at 10.07, 10.06 and 10.08 fps each, and 2 streams of
  4-chip DiTs at 17.53 fps each.
- **Only decode latency grows,** through queueing: 486, then 723, then 833 ms.
- **Frames are byte-identical,** alone or shared.
- **4 sessions hold 1.7 GiB,** and the decoder rank uses about 3 GiB in total.
- **Decoder replicas scale out:** 2 decoders serve 6 streams (placed 3 + 3), and 3 serve 9 (placed 3 + 3 + 3), all at
  10.00-10.07 fps.
- **Stable Audio (25 steps):** one decoder feeds 4 DiT jobs at almost full speed (14.13 → 14.44 s) and saturates at
  6. The model predicted about 4.5.

**7.5 Sensitivity to the stage ratio (Figure 9).** Stable Audio 3, 2-chip DiT, 1800 s of audio.

| Steps | Co-located | 2+1 | 2+2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 50 | 27.81 s | 27.02 s | 26.80 s |
| 25 | 14.88 s | 14.39 s | 14.16 s |
| 8 | 6.15 s | 5.48 s | 5.37 s |
| 4 | 4.07 s | 3.89 s | 3.32 s |
| 2 | 3.06 s | 3.75 s | 2.51 s |

We observe that:

- **The gain follows T_d / T_v.** It is small when the DiT dominates (many steps) and largest when the stages are
  balanced.
- **At 2 steps the decoder binds:** a 1-chip decoder loses and a 2-chip decoder wins 1.22x.
- **Rate matching works in both directions.**
- **LingBot at 2 steps (4-chip DiT, 480):** 23.5 fps co-located; 28.2 fps with 4 + 1 (1.20x, limited by the decoder,
  about 390 ms per chunk); 33.8 fps with 4 + 2 (1.44x). Fewer steps raise the decoder's share, so the gain grows if the
  decoder gets the chips. LingBot is distilled to 4 steps and cannot run more. The DiT-only mode (28.1 fps) syncs on
  every chunk and understates the DiT's pace at 2 steps.

**Fairness note.** All co-located numbers at 480 include the async frame emit (section 7.7); without it co-located
4 / 8 / 16 chips ran 14.3 / 20.0 / 23.4 fps. The 720p co-located 16-chip number is 11.4 fps with it (10.0 without).

**7.6 Latency (Figures 10 carwash.svg, 11 latency.svg).** The statement: splitting never makes a key press show
up sooner. With too few VAE chips it makes reactions about 0.1 s slower (4 + 1: 1221 vs 1114 ms). With enough VAE chips
the reaction time is the same as co-located (4 + 4: 1114 ms) while frames per second rise (17.6 vs 15.5). At 8 chips:
co-located 736 / 960 ms (22.6 fps) vs 8 + 1 881 / 1056 ms (25.7 fps): a 1-chip VAE again adds about 0.15 s. Press-to-photon over 100 presses, median / p90:

| Setup | fps | Median / p90 | Wait | DiT | Decode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Co-located 4 | 15.5 | 1114 / 1416 ms | 498 ms | 552 ms | 62 ms |
| 4+1 | 17.5 | 1221 / 1471 ms | 476 ms | 433 ms | 314 ms |
| 4+4 | 17.6 | 1114 / 1371 ms | 470 ms | 433 ms | 209 ms |

We observe that:

- **Decoder chips buy latency, not throughput.**
- **A decoder sized for latency** matches the co-located median, beats its p90, and keeps the 13% throughput gain.

**7.7 Ablations.**

| Ablation | Result |
|---|---|
| Disaggregated in one pod vs across pods | 17.51 vs 17.54 fps at 4+1; 25.74 vs 25.76 at 8+1. The gain is the decoupling, and pods cost nothing. |
| Per-session least-loaded placement vs sticky placement | 3/3/3 vs 4/4/1 sessions per decoder. Both at full speed at 9 streams, but sticky placement leaves one decoder near its limit (667 ms decode). |
| Async frame emit (JPEG off the critical path) | 14.2 → 15.5 fps co-located, byte-identical. It applies to every configuration. |
| Decoder compiler options scoped to the decoder vs process-wide | The 16-chip co-located DiT runs 299 vs 619 ms: the apparent "interference" was a configuration leak. |

**7.8 Resilience.**

- A decoder pod deleted, or its device tainted as faulty, in the middle of a stream: about 3 s to switch, 140 of 140
  chunks delivered, no pause over 1.5 s.
- With no decoder available, the session is refused (503).
- **Elasticity.** A second decoder replica added while 3 streams run takes 18 minutes from apply to Ready (setup and
  loading the compile cache). The streams that arrive after it run at full speed (10.06-10.07 fps), and 2 of the 3
  are placed on the new replica. Elastic decoders need a warm standby or a faster start; this is an operational
  limit, not a model limit.
- **Placement.** A 4 + 1 split with the decoder on the DiT's node (NeuronLink) vs on the other node (EFA): 17.58 vs
  17.60 fps; press-to-photon median 1216 vs 1231 ms, p90 1485 vs 1482 ms. The 1.1 MB hand-off (0.17 ms) makes
  placement free.

## 9 Conclusion

Two-stage generative pipelines are limited by the coupling of two stages with different bounds. Treating the stages
as independently sized, replicated and placed roles, an operating-system responsibility on EKS with DRA, EFA and
NIXL, recovers the chip time co-location spends on the wrong chip count. A cost model the operator computes at deploy time picks the sizing.
